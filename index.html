01

Mapping without the most expensive sensor on the vehicle

Archenaux Robotics · Primary engineer, sensor simulation and SLAM back end · 45 commits

A Doppler Velocity Log costs $8,710 and was the single most expensive item in the sensor budget, roughly four times the sonar and seventeen times the IMU. I helped validate that it is not required.

An IMU, a 2D mechanical scanning sonar, the known contour of the structure, and loop closure together produce a metrically accurate map. The back end is GTSAM iSAM2 with corner-based loop closure and GICP registration.

The interesting failure was that the graph diverged whenever a closure fired, which is the failure mode that makes a factor graph worse than dead reckoning. Separately, the registration acceptance threshold shipped at 0.15 and admitted bad matches. Raising it to 0.6 after characterizing the score distribution made the pipeline drift-free without tuning.

These numbers are from the Bremen MSS real-pool dataset, not the simulator. I also characterized the IMU that feeds it: a 30-minute stationary log, 321k samples, Allan variance, and Kalibr noise parameters. The first attempt was contaminated by a nearby speaker coupling into the MEMS accelerometer, which is the kind of thing you only find by looking at the FFT.

02

Training a detector with no real data to train on

Archenaux Robotics · Sole engineer, simulation and ML pipeline · 26 commits

Imagery of the target objects is classified or does not exist. So I generated it. A seeded procedural world generator emits ground-truth labels by 3D-to-2D projection, which means every frame is labeled for free and any scene is reproducible from its seed.

The difficulty is deliberate: seven object types, three turbidity levels, current drift, marine snow, kelp, and seabed distractors that a naive detector calls targets. The blocking problem was never recall, it was that the model flagged every barrel.

The evaluation also showed 0% recall on three object types, because the real-photo training set contained no flat or torpedo-shaped examples. I would rather publish that than a clean chart. An unevaluated detector is worse than none, because someone trusts it.

04

Bringing up a real eight-thruster ROV

Archenaux Robotics · Primary engineer, vehicle runtime and sensor bring-up · 52 commits

Archelon is a bespoke eight-thruster vehicle running ArduSub in a vectored six-degree-of-freedom configuration. I own the runtime that ships to it and I did the sensor bring-up, which is mostly a story about failures that look like software and are not.

The depth sensor read nothing no matter how it was wired, because BARO_PROBE_EXT was a bitmask missing the bit for the MS5837, so the autopilot never probed the I2C bus for it. The joystick moved every topic on the dashboard and no thrusters, because ArduPilot silently discards RC override from any system ID other than the one it is told to trust. The companion computer browned out under load, which turned out to be rail sag down the tether rather than thermal throttling, and is why the vehicle now carries a Pi 5 instead of a Jetson.

None of those are findable in simulation. I wrote the power-tree analysis that justified the companion swap, and it says plainly which of its figures are measured and which are estimated, because a number nobody can trace is worse than no number.

A second physics backend, and a test suite that argues with it

Archenaux Robotics · Sole engineer, NVIDIA Isaac Sim port

Porting the vehicle to a second physics engine: an animated Gerstner-wave ocean with underwater rendering, hydrodynamics covering buoyancy, damping, and added mass, and a bridge closing the control loop against Isaac physics.

Physics code is easy to write tests for and hard to write good ones for, because a wrong model still produces plausible motion. I used mutation testing to attack my own assertions until they were strict enough to notice, which surfaced distributed-buoyancy and NaN-propagation bugs before anyone else hit them.

The renders on this page were produced by the offscreen render path from that work, which exists so scene composition can be checked without a GPU-heavy interactive session.

06

An intercept simulation for a real client, and a film generated from code

Lytespace · Sole engineer, systems architecture and simulation · Client proposal, Chile

This existed to win a real client: a white-label perimeter surveillance system for a monitoring centre in Chile. I wrote the four-layer systems architecture and the costing behind the proposal, including the regulatory work on Chilean BVLOS permitting and the procurement constraints that ruled out most of the obvious camera vendors.

The technical core is an intercept simulation: ten ArduCopter instances armed in parallel against a radar model, tracked by an interacting-multiple-model estimator, with lead-pursuit guidance closing the loop on real autopilot firmware.

Then the film, because the buyer was not an engineer. Three Blender scene scripts define geometry, lighting, and camera language, and a Python assembler cuts the edit. Changing a shot means changing a parameter, not reopening a timeline.

About

I am early in my career and I have built eight robots. The ones I learned most from are the ones that broke in front of me.

It started with a 2D SLAM demo I thought meant I understood mobile robotics. It did not. Then a research internship in Malaysia, a maze solver, an ESP32 rover that taught me what real hardware costs, a final-year agricultural robot with a manipulator I built myself, and now an eight-thruster ROV that goes in the water.

Underwater is unforgiving in a useful way. You cannot talk your way past a localization error when the vehicle is down there and a tether is the only thing telling you what happened. The bugs are not in your code, they are in a bitmask, a rail voltage, or the refractive index of water.

The habit I care most about is measuring honestly. The evaluation harnesses I build are the ones that produce the numbers showing where my own models still fail, and I would rather publish those than a clean chart.

M.S. Robotics, Northeastern. Graduating 2027 and looking for robotics roles.
